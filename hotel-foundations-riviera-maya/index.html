Hotel and Resort Foundations in the Riviera Maya

For developers, owners and design teams building boutique hotels, resorts and condo-hotels in Cancún, Playa del Carmen, Tulum, Puerto Morelos, Cozumel and Costa Mujeres. The ground here is not the ground in a textbook: karst limestone with hidden cavities, a shallow and salty water table, and beach sand over rock along the coast. The foundation is where a hotel budget is won or lost, so we design it from real soil data, not from the neighbour’s drawings.

Hotel foundations, rule of thumb: on sound limestone a 3–5 storey hotel usually stands on isolated or strip footings with tie beams; poor or mixed ground and a high water table call for a mat foundation; towers on the coastal sand bar (Cancún Hotel Zone, Costa Mujeres) usually go to rock on piles. Foundations are typically 10–20% of the structural budget, and the geotechnical study decides which case you are in.

What We Do Below Ground

Geotechnical Study

Boreholes or rotary drilling to rock, lab tests, water-table level and chemistry, with a report signed for the DRO and the structural co-responsible.

Cavity Detection

Geophysics (electrical resistivity or ground-penetrating radar) plus probe drilling on a grid, to find caverns and soft pockets before the design is frozen.

Footings, Mats & Piles

Isolated and strip footings on limestone, mat foundations for poor ground, bored piles or micropiles to rock on sand and fill.

Water Table & Dewatering

Excavation below the water table with pumping or well points, waterproofed basements, cisterns and lift pits.

Marine-Grade Concrete

Low water/cement ratio, sulfate-resistant cement, larger cover to the steel and corrosion protection for salt groundwater and sea air.

Permits & Supervision

Structural calculations for the construction licence, DRO and structural co-responsible sign-off, inspections and concrete testing on site.

Why the ground in the Riviera Maya is different

The Yucatán Peninsula is a slab of karst limestone. Rain dissolves it from inside, which is how cenotes form. For a foundation that means:

  • Cavities you cannot see from the surface. A footing can sit on a thin crust over a void. Under a single house this is a risk; under a 60-key hotel with concentrated column loads it is the main risk.
  • Variable rock. Hard caprock (laja), soft sascab and pockets of clay can change within a few metres, so two columns of the same building may need different solutions.
  • A shallow, salty water table near the coast. It limits basements and cisterns, needs pumping during excavation, and attacks concrete and steel.
  • Beach sand over rock on the barrier islands and the coastal strip (Cancún Hotel Zone, Costa Mujeres, beachfront Playa del Carmen and Tulum): shallow footings on sand settle, so taller buildings go down to rock.

How we choose the foundation

We do not start from a foundation type. We start from the soil study and the loads:

  1. Geotechnical study with drilling to sound rock and the water table level.
  2. Cavity survey where the geology calls for it: geophysics over the footprint, then probe holes where the readings are suspicious.
  3. Load take-down from the architectural and structural design: number of floors, pools on roofs, water tanks, equipment.
  4. Options with cost and time: for example footings plus void filling versus a mat, or a mat versus piles, compared side by side before you approve.

Voids found during excavation are treated, not ignored: cleaned and filled with cyclopean or flowable concrete, grouted, or bridged with a beam or a thicker mat, as the structural engineer specifies.

Concrete that survives salt water

Foundations sit in the most aggressive part of the building: salty groundwater, humidity and, on the beachfront, sea spray. We specify dense, low water/cement concrete, sulfate-resistant cement where the water chemistry calls for it, generous cover to the reinforcement, proper curing and cylinder tests on every pour. Cutting corners here shows up 10–15 years later as cracked, rusting columns that are very expensive to repair in an operating hotel.
